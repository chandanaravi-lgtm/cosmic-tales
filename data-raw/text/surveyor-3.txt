 SURVEYOR 3
 NASA Lunar Exploration Mission  1967
What was Surveyor 3?
NASA’s Surveyor 3 was the second spacecraft in the Surveyor series to make a soft landing on the Moon. It
tested the lunar surface and helped scientists determine that the ground was strong enough to support the weight
of an Apollo Lunar Module.

In depth: the mission
Surveyor 3 was the third engineering flight in the Surveyor series. For the first time, a Surveyor carried a
surface-sampling instrument that could reach up to 5 feet (1.5 meters) from the lander and dig as deep as 7
inches (18 centimeters).
Unlike earlier Surveyor missions, Surveyor 3 began its journey from an Earth parking orbit. The Centaur upper
stage then fired its engine to send the spacecraft toward the Moon.
During descent, highly reflective rocks confused the lander’s descent radar. The main engine did not shut off at
the intended moment, at an altitude of about 14 feet (4.3 meters). Surveyor 3 bounced twice: first to about 33 feet
(10 meters), then to about 10 feet (3 meters). It finally settled into a soft landing at 00:04:17 UT on April 20, 1967,
in the southeastern region of Oceanus Procellarum, at 3° south latitude and 23.41° west longitude.
Less than an hour after landing, the spacecraft began transmitting images of its surroundings. Over the mission, it
sent 6,326 television pictures.
The surface sampler was used to dig trenches, test how firmly the soil could support a load, and move lunar
material in view of the television camera. Commands from Earth directed the probe to dig four trenches, perform
four bearing tests, and conduct thirteen impact tests.

The experiments indicated that lunar soil had a consistency similar to wet sand. Its bearing strength was about 10
pounds per square inch (0.7 kilograms per square centimeter, or 98 kilopascals). Scientists concluded that the
surface could support an Apollo Lunar Module.
The television camera
The camera used two lenses with focal lengths of 25 mm and 100 mm. It was mounted beneath a mirror that
could move horizontally and vertically. A single 200-line image took about 20 seconds to transmit. The system
could also produce 600-line images using digital picture transmission.
Apollo 12 visits Surveyor 3
More than three years later, on November 18, 1969, Apollo 12 astronauts Charles Conrad Jr. and Alan L. Bean
landed the Intrepid Lunar Module about 197 yards (180 meters) from the inactive Surveyor 3 spacecraft. During
their second moonwalk on November 19, the astronauts visited the lander and brought back several parts,
including the soil scoop and camera system. Scientists studied these components to learn how nearly two and a
half years of exposure to the lunar environment had affected them.
