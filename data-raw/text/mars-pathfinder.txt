 NASA Mars Exploration Mission  1996–1997
Mission overview
Mars Pathfinder was designed as a technology demonstration to deliver an instrumented lander and a free-ranging
robotic rover to the surface of Mars. The lander and its rover, Sojourner, carried instruments to study the Martian
atmosphere, climate, geology, rocks, and soil while testing new landing and surface-operation technologies.

Mission results
Mars Pathfinder returned about 2.3 billion bits of information before its final data transmission on September 27,
1997. It sent more than 16,500 lander images and 550 rover images, completed more than 15 chemical analyses of
rocks and soil, and collected extensive wind and weather data. Findings suggested Mars may once have been
warmer and wetter, with liquid water at the surface.
Airbag landing
After entering the Martian atmosphere, a parachute slowed Pathfinder's descent. A large airbag system cushioned
the impact—the first use of this landing technique on Mars.
The airbag-enclosed lander struck the surface at about 31 mph (14 meters per second). The system measured
about 19 feet (5.8 meters) in diameter. Pathfinder bounced about 15 times, reaching heights of up to 50 feet (15
meters), before coming to rest roughly two and a half minutes later, about six-tenths of a mile (1 kilometer) from the
initial impact point.
The Sojourner rover
The lander was named the Carl Sagan Memorial Station in honor of astronomer Carl Sagan. It deployed the rover
Sojourner, named after American civil-rights activist Sojourner Truth. Late on July 5, 1997 (sol 2, the second Martian
day), Sojourner rose to its full height of about 1 foot (30 centimeters) and rolled down the lander's rear ramp, which
was inclined at 20 degrees.
Landing site: Ares Vallis
Ares Vallis is an ancient floodplain in the northern hemisphere of Mars. Scientists selected it because it was
considered relatively safe for landing and contained a wide variety of rocks possibly deposited by a catastrophic
flood. Evidence suggested that a tremendous volume of water flowed through the region early in Mars' history.

Mars Pathfinder science highlights
 Evidence of ancient water: Rounded pebbles and cobbles suggested conglomerate rocks formed by flowing
water, supporting a warmer past when liquid water could remain stable.
 Mars' interior: Radio tracking helped measure Mars' location and pole of rotation. Results suggested a metallic
core radius greater than 800 miles (1,300 kilometers) but less than roughly 1,250 miles (2,000 kilometers).
 Magnetic dust: Airborne dust was magnetic. Its properties suggested the mineral could be maghemite, a strongly
magnetic form of iron oxide.
 Dust devils: Temperature, wind, and pressure sensors measured dust devils. The observations suggested these
gusts help mix dust into the atmosphere.
 Water-ice clouds: Early-morning water-ice clouds were observed in the lower atmosphere.
 Daily temperature changes: Abrupt morning temperature fluctuations suggested the surface warms the
atmosphere, with heat carried upward in small swirling air currents.
